# The theory of teaching a language

*(Language Harness, 2026-11-23. This adapts LearnHarness's [theory of learning and teaching](teaching-methodology.md) to languages; the prompts under `src/agent/prompts/` are a compression of it. It is the design intent, not a runtime specification or evidence of measured outcomes.)*

## 1. What "learned" means for a language

LearnHarness's terminal condition is that the goal understanding *feels obviously true*. A language is not a set of propositions to accept but a skill to run, so the condition becomes **availability**: the right form comes to mind in a situation the learner has not met before, sounds right to them, and comes out right — in comprehension (it is understood without translation effort) and in production (it is said or written without assembling it piece by piece).

A recited rule or a recognized word is not learned in this sense. It is memorized, and memorized forms decay unless they are used.

## 2. Two states, measured per strand

As in LearnHarness, a session runs between **current ability** and a **goal**, and the probe must define both precisely. For a language both are skill-shaped:

- The goal is a concrete *can-do*: order food in Mexico City, follow a Cantonese family dinner, read NHK Easy News, pass JLPT N4. It fixes the variety (Latin American or European Spanish), the channels (listening, speaking, reading, writing) and whether the script is in scope.
- Current ability is bracketed per strand — sound, script, vocabulary, grammar, comprehension, production — with a floor (what they reliably understand and produce) and a ceiling (where it breaks). Comprehension and production are measured separately: heritage speakers and long-time readers often understand far more than they can produce.

Probing uses real language — sentences to understand, build or hear — never questions *about* the language that someone who doesn't speak it could answer. Total beginners are not probed in the target language at all; the probe measures what transfers from the languages they already know.

## 3. The click — memorized pieces collapsing into generators

LearnHarness's click is loose facts collapsing into a structure generated by a few base facts. Language has the same moment: separately memorized chunks — *me gusta*, *te gusta*, *nos gustan* — collapse into one pattern (*gustar* means "to please"; the liked thing is the subject), and suddenly sentences the learner never saw become producible. Teaching aims at generators, not lists.

## 4. One new element per step

The atomic unit of teaching is a **step that adds exactly one new element** — a word or chunk, a form, a structure, a sound — to language the learner already understands. This is LearnHarness's "one reasoning step at a time" in its language form, and it coincides with the comprehensible-input principle: input with one unknown among knowns is understood, and understood input is what gets acquired. Two consequences carry over unchanged:

- **Every landing is easy.** The new element is the only unknown, so its meaning is forced by everything around it.
- **Errors surface immediately.** A check after each step uses the new element in a new sentence; a wrong form is caught at the first sentence, before it is built into every later one.

## 5. Laying down and noticing

LearnHarness's two placement moves — foundations accepted as-is, and derivations that make facts feel discovered — become:

- **Core patterns, laid down plainly.** Some things a language simply is (a sound, the basic word order, that nouns have gender). State them unconditionally at the learner's level; an exception they will not meet for weeks is a later step, not a caveat now. Universal statements ("every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine") lock in fastest; a real definition beats a list of uses.
- **Noticing instead of derivation.** A form feels arbitrary when nothing showed why it had to be that way. So examples come before the rule: a few sentences that share the pattern and differ in one feature, or a minimal pair that isolates it (*Der Hund sieht den Mann* / *Den Hund sieht der Mann*: same words, same order, and the articles alone decide who sees whom). The learner sees the pattern working, then it is named — or they name it.
- **The learner's own language as a lever.** Where it works the same way, that is free structure; where it differs, the difference is named before it becomes a transfer error.

## 6. Words, sound and script

- Vocabulary is chosen by frequency and by the goal, taught in the chunks it lives in, glossed in the learner's language but carried by context, and recycled in later steps — a word met once is not learned.
- Sounds the learner's language lacks are taught explicitly and heard (every phrase the learner should hear is played from the device's voice). In tonal languages the tone belongs to every word from the first.
- A script is its own strand, introduced progressively with a fading romanization when reading is part of the goal.

## 7. Checks and practice

Checks follow every step, as in LearnHarness, and prefer production to recognition: say this, complete this, answer in the target language. Multiple-choice options are each the answer of a specific wrong model — the wrong gender, the learner's own word order, the false friend — so a miss names the misconception.

Practice keeps LearnHarness's retrieval theory (inactive targets, decision points per minute, the ~85% band per retrieval event, interleaving, minimal hints, retests in fresh surfaces) with language-shaped modes:

| Mode | Language form |
| --- | --- |
| Recall | Produce a word or form from a cue: a translation prompt, a gap, a situation |
| Discrimination | Choose between confusables: *ser*/*estar*, *por*/*para*, two past tenses, two tones |
| Composition | Produce a whole utterance under load: a dialogue turn, a description, a reply to a message |

Listening (a hidden `speak` block) and speaking (a dictated answer) raise difficulty in any mode.

## 8. What this demands of the implementation

1. Every session knows its target and explanation languages, and every agent is told them.
2. The learner hears what they are learning: audio is available wherever target-language text appears, and listening items can hide their text.
3. Speech input can transcribe the target language when the learner practices speaking.
4. Grading accepts every correct, natural rendering and separates meaning errors, form errors, spelling slips and transcription noise.
5. Any language can be taught; what the app cannot support for a language (audio, dictation) degrades honestly rather than pretending.
