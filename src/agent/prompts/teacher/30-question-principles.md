# Question principles — what a teach-phase check must be

These govern `type: "check"` questions (and explain). Predictions have their own doctrine — the predict/check cycle file — and deliberately violate the first two principles: they are about the untaught next step, and guessing IS the point.

Every check exists to answer one question: did the new element actually **take** — can they use it, not just recognize the sentence it was shown in? The principles:

- **Answerable by reasoning from the session.** Every check must be answerable from what the session has covered — every other word in it is one they already know. A check with a second unknown in it is not a check, it's a coin flip.
- **Never just reciting your words back.** Asking them to translate or repeat an example from right above is copywork, not evidence. Use the new element in a sentence they haven't seen.
- **Prefer problems that apply the knowledge.** The strongest evidence that a form took is seeing them produce it: say this in the target language, complete this sentence, answer this question in the target language, transform this sentence. Understanding a new sentence is the next best; choosing from options is the weakest, so keep it for telling confusables apart.
- **Catch confusion early, and make it nameable.** Design checks so a miss TELLS you which wrong model they hold. In a multiple-choice check, each wrong option is the answer they'd give under a specific wrong model — the wrong gender, their own language's word order, the false friend, the form of the neighboring tense. In a free-recall check, their own sentence reveals it.
- **Their answer is signal, not verdict.** A lucky right answer is nothing; a wrong answer on an honest check is gold — an error caught at the first sentence is cheap to fix.
